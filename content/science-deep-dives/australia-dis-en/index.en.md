**“When Best Practice Fails”.** Australia has been lauded as a global exemplar in tobacco control, consistently setting international regulatory norms through standardized packaging and aggressive public health campaigns. However, this reputation masks a profound strategic failure. By dogmatically pursuing "best practice" policies endorsed by the World Health Organization—specifically punitive excise taxation and a restrictive medical-only access model for nicotine vapes—Australia has inadvertently triggered a crisis of domestic sovereignty. The state has effectively abdicated its regulatory duty of care, ceding control of the market to criminal syndicates.

{{< figure src="Slide2.en.jpg" class="w-[400px]" >}}

In the lexicon of modern policy, "**Tobacco Control**" is the suite of institutional actors and instruments designed to reduce use. Yet, the current Australian approach has birthed a strategic irony: smoked tobacco—scientifically the most harmful delivery mechanism for nicotine—remains the only non-medicinal form of nicotine legally available. This "nicotine elimination" strategy mirrors the 1920s U.S. Alcohol Prohibition, creating a vacuum that organized crime has filled with industrial efficiency.

{{< figure src="Slide3.en.jpg" class="w-[400px]" >}}

This situation has been comprehensively analyzed in a recent paper by [Borland et al](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/add.70428), published in the journal Addiction, and this analysis is presented here.\
\
As the authors argue in their seminal appraisal:

"*Current policy has achieved precisely this outcome—ceding control of the nicotine market to criminal enterprises who decide what nicotine products are sold, at what price and under what conditions. Tobacco control has indeed lost control.*"

This shift does more than just move sales underground; it fundamentally undermines the public health goals it was designed to achieve. In this prohibition-style environment, price signals are lost, product safety is non-existent, and youth access is managed by criminals rather than regulated retailers. This fiscal and social hemorrhage is not a byproduct of policy; it is a direct consequence of a strategy that has lost touch with market realities.

**The AUD \$7.2 Billion Shadow Economy**

The engine of this illicit market is the widening price gap created by extreme excise taxes. Between 1990 and 2024, the price of a standard cigarette pack increased sevenfold, exceeding AUD \$40. By 2025, the tax component alone reached nearly AUD \$30 per pack. This fiscal environment has provided organized crime with a 1500% profit margin, rendering traditional law enforcement "frictions"—such as seizures or arrests—merely a variable cost of doing business rather than a meaningful deterrent.

**Market Comparison: Legal vs. Illicit**

| **Regulatory Domain** | **Legal Market** | **Illicit Market (Black Market)** | **Advantage** |
|----|----|----|----|
| **Price & Taxation** | High taxes (Excise + GST); AUD \$40+ per pack. | Avoids taxes; approximately AUD \$30 cheaper. | **Illicit** |
| **Product Standards** | Mandatory safety/quality compliance. | No standards; risk of dangerous adulterants. | **Illicit (Cost)** |
| **Packaging** | Standardized/Plain; large health warnings. | Unrestricted; branded; available as "singles." | **Illicit** |
| **Flavors/Variety** | Highly restricted. | No restrictions on flavors or product types. | **Illicit** |

| **Consumer Willingness** | High legal/social barriers; high cost. | Price-sensitive users and youth gravitate here. | **Illicit (Demand)** |

The economic scale of this failure is catastrophic. National tobacco excise revenue has plummeted by 57%, representing a fiscal hemorrhage of over **AUD \$7.7 billion**. Crucially, this lost revenue is **more than twice the entire national drug law enforcement budget**, representing a staggering budgetary irony: the state is losing more in potential revenue than it spends on policing all illicit drugs combined.

By financial value, the illicit nicotine market (estimated at **AUD \$7.2 billion**) now dominates the criminal landscape:

1.  **Methamphetamine** (AUD \$8.9 billion)

2.  **Illicit Nicotine** (AUD \$7.2 billion)

3.  **Cocaine, Heroin, MDMA, and Cannabis** (The nicotine market exceeds the worth of these four markets combined).

**Beyond the Ledger: The Criminal and Social Toll**

The illicit nicotine trade has evolved into an asymmetric warfare scenario, sparking "Tobacco Wars" that threaten both community safety and the integrity of national infrastructure. The capital generated by this market is not static; it funds a broader ecosystem of serious organized crime.

**Systemic Violence and Criminal Consequences**

- **Arson Attacks:** Since 2023, over 270 arson incidents have targeted retail premises nationally—acts used to extort shopkeepers or eliminate competitors.

- **Extreme Violence:** The market is increasingly characterized by homicides, kidnappings, torture, and robberies used to enforce criminal contracts.

- **Secondary Financing:** Nicotine profits provide the liquidity for sex trafficking, weapons smuggling, and the expansion of other illicit drug networks.

**National Security and Institutional Integrity**

The sheer scale of the profits invites systemic corruption. The Australian Criminal Intelligence Commission has warned of "malicious insiders" within the transportation sector who facilitate mass importation. Furthermore, as enforcement duties shift toward health departments and local councils—bodies that lack the rigorous internal integrity units of police forces—the risk of bribery and institutional compromise grows. By insisting on a "medical-only" vaping model, the government has effectively gifted the recreational market to organized crime, turning a public health regulation into a permanent revenue stream for criminal kingpins.

{{< figure src="Slide10.en.jpg" class="w-[400px]" >}}

**The Enforcement Delusion: Why Supply Reduction is Failing**

The Australian government has allocated over AUD \$340 million to combat illicit nicotine through supply reduction. However, historical data suggests this is a strategic delusion. Between 2009 and 2021, drug law enforcement spending tripled, and seizures hit record highs; yet, the purity of methamphetamine and cocaine increased while prices dropped.

The failure of supply reduction in this context is driven by the **Central Paradox**: Intensified policing makes the illicit market more covert and dangerous but does not remove the underlying demand. In high-volume markets, the distribution network is too dense to be dismantled.

- **Population Density:** The successful heroin "drought" of the early 2000s targeted a market involving only 0.2% of the population. Nicotine, by contrast, is used by **10% of Australian adults**.

- **Market Displacement:** Because there is no legal recreational pathway for vapes, enforcement disproportionately impacts the safer alternative. This drives users back to the high-priced legal cigarette market or the covert criminal cigarette trade, perversely encouraging the most harmful form of consumption.

**A Tale of Two Neighbors: The New Zealand Alternative**

New Zealand serves as a critical control group, proving that market control is possible through harm reduction rather than prohibition. While both nations employ high tobacco taxes, their divergence on Alternative Nicotine Products (ANPs) has created starkly different outcomes.

**Comparative Analysis (2016–2023):**

- **Regulatory Model:** Australia treats vapes as medical products (prescription only); New Zealand regulates them as adult consumer products with health-oriented oversight.

- **Illicit Market:** New Zealand’s illicit tobacco market is proportionally much smaller because legal, regulated alternatives provide a "pressure-release valve" for consumer demand.

- **Smoking Decline:** New Zealand has seen a faster decline in adult smoking prevalence than Australia.

- **Health Inequalities:** NZ's model has specifically benefited Indigenous (Māori) and disadvantaged populations, who have utilized vapes to transition away from smoked tobacco at higher rates.

New Zealand’s experience suggests that providing a legal, competitively priced pathway for adults to access lower-risk products is the only effective way to dismantle the black market’s dominance.

**Conclusion: A Roadmap to Regaining Control**

Australia must move from an ideological pursuit of "nicotine elimination" to a rational appraisal of market dynamics. The "effective impossibility" of a nicotine-free society must be acknowledged. The current choice for policymakers is not between nicotine use and no nicotine use, but between a market controlled by the state or one controlled by organized crime.

**Strategic Policy Recommendations**

1.  **Immediate Tax Freeze:** Halt all further tobacco excise increases to stop the widening price gap that fuels criminal profit margins.

2.  **Consumer Regulation of ANPs:** Transition vapes and other lower-risk alternatives to an adult consumer product model. This allows legal retailers to compete with the black market on price, variety, and safety.

3.  **Evidence Realignment:** Conduct an authoritative re-review of vaping harms to correct the community misperception that vapes are as harmful as cigarettes—a myth that currently traps smokers in the most lethal form of addiction.

{{< figure src="Slide15.en.jpg" class="w-[400px]" >}}

To regain control, Australia requires regulation that is proportionate to risk and manageable in the face of persistent demand. Only by legitimizing a low-risk nicotine market can the state bankrupt the criminal syndicates and realign its public health, fiscal, and national security objectives.

**Source:** Borland R, Martin J, Jegasothy E, Youdan B, Hall W. Has Australia lost control of its tobacco and nicotine markets? Addiction. 2026 Apr 21. doi: 10.1111/add.70428.
