**It's Often the Road Out of Smoking.** If you smoke some days and vape on others - or vape while still having the occasional cigarette - you have probably been told this proves vaping doesn't work. Even worse, many claim that this is even worse than just smoking. Public health critics present dual use as evidence of failure or as added risk. The science tells a different story: for many people, dual use is a transitional stage on the way to complete switching.

{{< figure src="slide2.en.jpg" class="w-[400px]" >}}

## What the Data Show

Analysis of England's Smoking Toolkit Study ([Jackson et al., 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39842468/)) found something striking: between 2016 and 2024, the pattern of dual use shifted from 'daily smoking plus occasional vaping' toward 'occidental smoking plus daily vaping.' That direction matters enormously - it describes people progressively loosening cigarettes' grip rather than the reverse. Toxicant research ([Xue et al., 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12370462/)) confirms that harm among dual users is driven primarily by how much they continue to smoke: those who substantially cut down cigarettes move closer to the biomarker profiles of people who quit entirely.

And this makes perfect sense. If you smoked 20 cigarettes per day before vaping initiation, and you now switch to 5 cigarettes per day and use vapes, **it is impossible** to be exposed to the same or higher level of toxicants than before; you definitely experience lower toxicant exposure.

However, even smoking few cigarettes per day results in much higher toxicant exposure compared to completely quitting (even if you quit by vaping daily); thus, **complete cigarette cessation should be the main goal for every smoker.**

{{< figure src="slide3.en.jpg" class="w-[400px]" >}}

## Vaping After Quitting Protects Against Relapse

Perhaps the most practically important finding comes from [Hajek and colleagues (2026)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41560608/), analyzing data from a large UK stop-smoking trial: continuing to vape after quitting smoking was associated with significantly lower risk of relapse over twelve months. This challenges the assumption that people should stop vaping as soon as they stop smoking. Sustained use of a far less harmful product is better understood as relapse prevention than as a new problem.

{{< figure src="slide4.en.jpg" class="w-[400px]" >}}

## What This Means for You

If you are currently using both products, the goal is clear: keep pushing in one direction. Every cigarette you don't smoke matters more than anything else about your pattern of use. Complete switching produces dramatic reductions in toxicant exposure; cutting down helps proportionally; and continued vaping after your last cigarette appears to help you stay free of them. Don't let anyone tell you that imperfect progress is no progress - but equally, **complete switching** is where the real health gains lie, so treat every cigarette-free stretch as a target to extend.

Sources: Jackson et al., Addiction 2025 \| Xue et al., Nicotine Tob Res 2025 \| Hajek et al., Addiction 2026
